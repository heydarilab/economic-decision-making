Adjustable-rate mortgage: the worst case, one year at a time

Economic Decision Making class example. A $500,000 adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) at 6.5% a year, compounded monthly, over 30 years. The rate resets once a year and can rise by at most one percentage point at each reset. This example takes the worst case: every reset goes up by the full point. The loan is paid off at the end of year 5. Step through to see how each year's payment is found, then what the whole loan is worth to a borrower whose time value of money is 9%.

Notation and assumptions
Pk
Balance owed at the start of year k. It is the principal the payment is re-priced on that year. P1 = $500,000.
Ak
Monthly payment during year k.
ik
Monthly rate in year k: the nominal annual rate divided by 12.
Nk
Months remaining at the start of year k: 360, 348, 336, 324, 312.
B60
Balance paid off at month 60, on top of the 60th regular payment.
j
Borrower's monthly time value of money: 9% ÷ 12 = 0.75%.
Vk
Worth of year k's 12 payments at the start of year k (month 12(k−1)), discounted at j.
PWk
Vk moved back to month 0.
PWB
The payoff B60 moved back to month 0.
(A/P, i, N)
i(1+i)N / [(1+i)N − 1], the capital recovery factor.
(P/A, i, N)
1 / (A/P, i, N), the present worth of N equal payments.
(P/F, i, n)
(1+i)−n, the present worth of a single amount n months away.

Worst case: the rate rises by its 1-point annual cap at every reset. All rates are nominal annual rates compounded monthly, including the 9% TVOM. Values are computed without rounding intermediate steps, so hand calculations with rounded factors may differ slightly.
